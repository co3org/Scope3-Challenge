> Every one of these is a real pattern seen in corporate Scope 3 programmes. Each entry gives the mistake, why it matters, and the fix. Grouped into four themes: measurement, credibility, action, and strategy.

## Measurement Mistakes

**1. Using spend-based factors for your most material category.**
*Why it matters:* spend-based factors can be off by ±100–400%, and the error is directional (cheap, high-carbon suppliers get undercounted).
*The fix:* get primary data where the footprint is largest; use spend only for the long tail.

**2. Treating a total Scope 3 number as if it were precise.**
*Why it matters:* the number is a model output, not a measurement. Presenting it to two decimal places implies a false precision.
*The fix:* state the method, the uncertainty range, and the dominant assumptions.

**3. Ignoring the emission-factor *vintage* and *geography*.**
*Why it matters:* a factor for "steel" that is five years old or US-specific can be wrong by 2–3x for your actual supply chain.
*The fix:* record source, year, and geography for every factor; prefer factors matching your suppliers' location.

**4. Mixing emission-factor databases without reconciling boundaries.**
*Why it matters:* USEEIO, Exiobase, Ecoinvent, and DEFRA use different system boundaries — results aren't comparable.
*The fix:* use one primary database per calculation and document it (see [02 Complexity](02_complexity_challenges.md)).

**5. Omitting Category 11 because it's "hard to estimate."**
*Why it matters:* for automotive, energy, and electronics, Cat 11 is often the *majority* of the footprint. Omitting it is omitting the main event.
*The fix:* model it with clearly stated lifetime, energy-use, and grid assumptions (see [09 Worked Example](09_worked_example.md)).

**6. Counting the same emission twice *within* your own inventory.**
*Why it matters:* e.g., fuel that is Scope 1 can also appear in Cat 3 — a classic intra-company double count.
*The fix:* map each activity to exactly one scope/category and document the mapping.

**7. Forgetting that a restatement changes your base year.**
*Why it matters:* if methodology changes, your baseline must be restated or trends become meaningless.
*The fix:* restate the base year on any material methodology change and disclose it.

## Credibility Mistakes

**8. Claiming "carbon neutral" on the back of cheap offsets.**
*Why it matters:* the 2023–2024 VCM scandals collapsed trust in low-quality offsets, and SBTi/VCMI now restrict their use.
*The fix:* only use offsets for residual emissions, with integrity labels (ICVCM), and say so precisely (see [06 Standards](06_standards_regulatory.md)).

**9. Confusing "avoided emissions" with "reductions."**
*Why it matters:* avoided emissions are a counterfactual claim — they didn't necessarily reduce anything measurable.
*The fix:* report reductions against a real baseline; label avoided emissions separately and never as a footprint reduction.

**10. Ignoring additionality.**
*Why it matters:* a reduction that would have happened anyway (or is legally required) isn't a genuine credit.
*The fix:* apply financial, regulatory, and barrier tests — see [04 Abatement Challenges](04_abatement_challenges.md).

**11. Letting the supplier sell a credit *and* claim the reduction in its own Scope 1.**
*Why it matters:* double attribution of the same tonne. The supplier must retire the claim when it sells.
*The fix:* require a registration-and-retirement system with a unique identifier per credit.

**12. Not getting third-party verification.**
*Why it matters:* self-declared reductions won't survive CSRD assurance or investor scrutiny.
*The fix:* independent verification to ISO 14064-3 (see [05 Carbon3 Solution](05_carbon3_solution.md)).

**13. Hiding behind "we follow the GHG Protocol."**
*Why it matters:* the Protocol is voluntary and allows methodology choices that swing numbers dramatically.
*The fix:* disclose *which* choices you made, not just that you "follow the Protocol."

## Action Mistakes

**14. Equating a supplier engagement target with a reduction.**
*Why it matters:* "65% of suppliers have targets" is an *activity* metric — it doesn't measure whether emissions actually fell.
*The fix:* pair engagement with a financing mechanism and track actual supplier emissions.

**15. Asking suppliers for data without offering anything back.**
*Why it matters:* disclosure is a cost and a competitive risk to the supplier; one-sided requests get silence.
*The fix:* offer value — preferential financing, longer contracts, or co-investment (see [04 Abatement Challenges](04_abatement_challenges.md)).

**16. Divesting from high-emitting suppliers instead of financing their transition.**
*Why it matters:* divestment moves the emissions to a less-scrutinised balance sheet; the planet doesn't care.
*The fix:* prefer transition finance and engagement for the suppliers you can realistically influence.

**17. Spending the whole budget on the cheapest abatement.**
*Why it matters:* the cheapest abatement (energy efficiency, renewables) is real but often already underway — it's not additional and won't close the gap alone.
*The fix:* sequence from cheapest to hardest, but build a path to the hard-to-abate (see [04](04_abatement_challenges.md)).

**18. Treating insetting as a magic bullet.**
*Why it matters:* inset credits are promising but face unresolved accounting (GHG Protocol) and additionality risks.
*The fix:* use insets for genuine in-value-chain reductions, with verification — not as a compliance shortcut (see [05 Carbon3 Solution](05_carbon3_solution.md)).

**19. Ignoring the free-rider problem in multi-buyer supply chains.**
*Why it matters:* if you fund a supplier's solar panels, your competitors benefit from the same Scope 3 improvement without paying.
*The fix:* exclusive allocation, co-funding agreements, or contract structures that share the cost.

## Strategy & Disclosure Mistakes

**20. Setting a Scope 3 target you cannot influence.**
*Why it matters:* an absolute Cat 11 target that depends on grid decarbonisation and customer behaviour is a commitment you can't keep.
*The fix:* separate what you control (product efficiency, EV mix) from what you don't (grid, adoption), and set targets accordingly.

**21. Disclosing Scope 3 without a transition plan.**
*Why it matters:* regulators and investors increasingly want *how*, not just *what*. A number without a plan is a liability.
*The fix:* attach levers, capital, and milestones (see [06 Standards](06_standards_regulatory.md)).

**22. Ignoring the GHG Protocol revision.**
*Why it matters:* it will decide whether buyers can claim supplier reductions — a make-or-break question for inset accounting.
*The fix:* track it actively; don't build a multi-year strategy on an unsettled rule.

**23. Treating Scope 3 as the sustainability team's problem.**
*Why it matters:* the levers live in procurement, product design, and finance — the sustainability team can't pull them alone.
*The fix:* put carbon-aware data into procurement and design decisions (see [00 Executive Summary](00_executive_summary.md)).

**24. Reporting only a total, with no category breakdown.**
*Why it matters:* a total is useless for decision-making — you can't act on a number you can't decompose. (Yet 42% of disclosing companies do exactly this.)
*The fix:* disclose the category breakdown, at least for material categories.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The most damaging mistakes are **structural, not arithmetic**: spend-based material categories, avoided-emissions claims, engagement-as-reduction.
- **Disclose methods and assumptions** — the number is only as good as the transparency around it.
- **Pair measurement with a financing mechanism**, or nothing actually reduces.
